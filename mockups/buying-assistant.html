Buying assistant — 4 directions for the paint-first workflow

Exploration only. Nothing here is built; this is the mockup review step that precedes a PLAN.md entry, in the manner of studio-layouts.html and equiv-source-picker.html.

The observation (Ryan, in use): the app gets opened less to pick a scheme and more to search paints and find what is close to one — a buying assistant.

What that actually is: not a new persona. It is P3 · Marcus, the Range Switcher, already in USE_CASES.md §1 — “give me the closest paint I can actually buy today for this colour.” The app has every piece of P3's answer (ΔE matching, cross-brand equivalents, owned/to-buy marks, the interchangeable groups) but files them all inside the scheme output. To ask “what is close to Mephiston Red?” today you must pick a paint, let it seed a scheme, then switch to the Equivalents tab. The engine is fine; the information architecture assumes PLAN is the job when in practice the job is RESOLVE → SHOP (§0.5).

So this is a priority inversion between existing personas, not a missing feature — which is good news: three of the four directions below are re-routing, not new engine work.

A · “Find” as a third top-level mode new surface · additive

Studio / Shelf becomes Studio / Shelf / Find. A search-first surface whose whole output is “this paint, and the paints near it”. No seed, no harmony, no roles — the scheme engine is never started. Maps cleanly onto a future app tab bar (§3.6).

A third mode is a real cost on a phone, and it overlaps the Shelf — both are “a list of paints with search and filters”.

B · Paint detail sheet, reachable from everywhere no new mode · smallest

Tapping any paint anywhere — drawer chip, Shelf cell, live-palette column, a ladder rung, an equivalents row — opens one sheet that answers everything about that paint. Every existing surface becomes a doorway to the buying answer, and nothing moves.

C · Substitute finder — “I need X, what can I use?” the literal ask

The shopping-gap flow inverted. You name a paint you don't have — a tutorial's recipe, a discontinued pot, something a friend used — and constrain the answer by what is actually available to you: only what you own, or only the brands the shop in front of you stocks.

A brand filter is a crude proxy for a shop's actual stock, and we have no store data. It is honest only if labelled as a brand filter, not “what this shop has”.

D · Flip the default — the paint list is the home biggest change · no new surface

Take the observation at face value: if the job is mostly paints, open on paints. The Shelf grid becomes Paints — the whole catalogue, with owned/to-buy as filters rather than the point — and the Studio becomes something you launch from a paint you have chosen.

Cross-cutting · Scan a pot in the shop v2 · native

Point the camera at a pot and get: do I already own this · what is it close to · is it on my list. It is the in-store half of directions C and D. The camera makes it a native-app capability (IOS_APP_PLAN.md) — BarcodeDetector exists in Chrome/Android but not Safari, so the web build would be Android-only at best.

Mephiston Red
Citadel · Base — matched from the label ✓ you own this

The blocker is data, not the camera. Two routes, and only one of them is available to us today:

RouteWhat it needsFeasible now?
Barcode (EAN/UPC) A barcode → paint-id mapping. paints.json has no such field, and no open dataset carries one; it would have to be gathered pot by pot. No. A scanner with no mapping scans a number and returns nothing.
Label OCR Read the printed name, fuzzy-match it against the 2,508 names we already hold. On-device Vision text recognition on iOS; no new data at all. Yes — and it degrades gracefully: a bad read just puts text in the search field.
Crowd-sourced barcodes Scan-and-tag: users map a barcode to a paint they identify by hand, building the table over time. No — needs a backend to collect it, which §1 forbids.

Verdict.

OCR first. It works with the dataset we have, keeps the no-backend rule, and reaches the same goal — identify the pot in my hand.
Barcode is the better UX if the mapping ever exists; treat it as a data-sourcing project (TD5's sibling), not a UI one.

Recommendation — B, then C, and hold D

The four are not exclusive. B is the leaf node all of them need, and it is small enough to ship on its own and learn from.

  1. B — the paint detail sheet. One new component, reusing equivalents(), nearestPaints(), groupMembers() and the store marks — all shipped. It makes the Shelf's 2,508 swatches answer questions instead of just being marked, and it is the reversible first step: if the observation is right, usage will show it.
  2. C — the substitute finder, as a mode on the sheet rather than a new surface (“Find substitutes” in the mock above), with the own/buy/brand constraints. This is the literal buying assistant, and needs no engine work.
  3. A — “Find” mode only if B and C prove that search wants a home of its own. Adding a third mode first would be guessing.
  4. D — flipping the default is the one to sit on. It is the most honest reading of the observation and the easiest to regret: the scheme engine is the differentiator, and one person's usage over a few weeks is a signal, not a mandate. Revisit once B/C have been lived with.
  5. Scanner — OCR route, native only, after the Stage-1 app shell exists. Park the barcode variant behind the data question.

The through-line: every direction here is about routing, not capability. The app already computes every answer P3 needs — it just files them behind a scheme.
